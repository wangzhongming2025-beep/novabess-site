Battery Peak Shaving: How Time-of-Use Arbitrage Turns Cheap Night Power Into Savings

Peak ShavingTOUArbitrageHome ESS

Electricity is not one price all day — it is cheap overnight and at midday, and expensive in the early-evening peak. A home battery lets you buy low and use your own stored power when prices spike. That simple move, repeated every day, is "peak shaving" or "time-of-use arbitrage," and in the right markets it quietly pays a battery back.

Bottom line: Peak shaving works wherever a time-of-use or dynamic tariff creates a real peak/off-peak spread. A 10 kWh battery on one daily cycle saves roughly $800-1,400/year in strong markets (Australia, UK, parts of the EU and US). You do not need solar — grid charging alone captures the spread. The catch: the inverter must support scheduled AC grid charging, and the payoff lives or dies by the size of your local price gap.

What "peak shaving" and "arbitrage" actually mean

Most homes pay a flat rate per kWh, so when you use power does not matter. But a growing share of households are on time-of-use (TOU) or dynamic tariffs, where the unit price changes by the hour. The pattern is consistent: overnight and sunny midday are cheap, and the early-evening window (roughly 4-9 pm, when everyone cooks, streams and charges) is the most expensive part of the day.

Peak shaving means serving that expensive evening load from your battery instead of the grid, so the peak is "shaved" off your bill. Arbitrage is the money version of the same idea: you buy electricity at the cheap rate, store it, and avoid buying at the expensive rate. The battery is a small, automated hedge against peak pricing.

How it works, in four steps

  1. Switch to a TOU / dynamic tariff. Your supplier separates cheap off-peak windows from expensive peak windows. Without this price spread there is nothing to capture.
  2. Charge during the cheap window. Overnight (or midday on some plans) the battery fills from the grid — or from your solar if you have it — at the low rate.
  3. Discharge during the peak. From about 4-9 pm the battery powers the home instead of the grid, so you avoid the high rate entirely.
  4. Repeat every day. One full cycle a day is typical. Over a year the small daily spread compounds into hundreds of dollars or pounds.

No solar is required. Solar only adds a second, free source of cheap energy to store — but a grid-charged battery captures the arbitrage on its own. If you are weighing the pure-grid case, our no-solar backup + arbitrage calculator shows the payback with no panels at all.

The savings formula (and a worked example)

Annual saving = (peak − off-peak) × usable kWh × efficiency × cycles/day × 365

Which countries is peak shaving best for?

Arbitrage needs three things: a TOU or dynamic tariff, a meaningful peak/off-peak spread, and a battery that can charge from the grid on a schedule. Here is the 2026 lay of the land:

TierCountriesWhy
T1 — strongAustralia, UK, Germany, USA (CA/HI/MA/NY)Large spreads, mature TOU/dynamic tariffs, high retail prices. Australia and the UK lead on published savings.

T3 — weak for arbitrageSouth Africa, Spain, UAEBackup (load shedding) and solar self-consumption dominate; the price spread is not the main driver, so arbitrage is secondary.

Two cautions from the field. First, in Germany the savings depend on the smart-meter rollout — some households waited 8-14 months for their DSO to install the intelligent measurement system (iMSys) before dynamic pricing could start. Check meter status before quoting arbitrage. Second, the inverter must actually support AC grid charging in TOU mode; a documented case saw a quoted €700/year collapse to ~€90 because the installed inverter could self-consume solar but not charge from the grid on schedule.

What you need (and the one spec that makes or breaks it)

  • A battery with an EMS or hybrid inverter that supports scheduled AC grid charging. Verify the spec line "AC grid charging (TOU mode)" — this is the single point of failure for arbitrage.
  • A TOU or dynamic tariff from a supplier that offers one (Octopus, Tibber, aWATTar, and most EU majors now do).
  • A smart meter so the supplier can bill half-hourly and you can see the spread.
  • Right-sized capacity. 7-12 kWh usable fits most homes; 12-18 kWh if you also run an EV and heat pump. Oversizing past that band leaves capacity idle on shoulder-season days and weakens the per-kWh return.

Frequently asked questions

What is battery peak shaving?

Peak shaving is using stored battery energy during the most expensive hours of the day so you draw less (or no) power from the grid when rates are high. A home battery charged off-peak shaves the peak off your daily bill — the load is still served, just from the battery instead of the grid.

How does time-of-use arbitrage work with a home battery?

On a time-of-use (TOU) tariff, electricity is cheap overnight or at midday and expensive in the early-evening peak. The battery charges during the cheap window (from the grid, or from solar) and discharges from roughly 4-9 pm when prices spike. Each day you buy low and avoid buying high — that spread is the arbitrage profit.

How much can a home battery save with peak shaving?

A 10 kWh usable battery on one cycle a day saves roughly (peak - off-peak) x 10 x 0.9 efficiency x 365 per year. With a $0.25/kWh spread that is about $820/year. Real-world published figures run $900-1,400/yr in Australia, ~£690/yr in the UK on Octopus Go, and €400+/yr in Ireland once dynamic tariffs arrive — the spread and the number of cycles drive the result.

Which countries are best for battery peak shaving arbitrage?

The strongest 2026 markets are Australia (South Australia and Western Australia peak/off-peak spreads of 30-45¢/kWh), the UK (Octopus Go 7p vs 26p), Germany (Tibber/aWATTar dynamic pricing, but watch the smart-meter wait), the USA (California, Hawaii, Massachusetts, New York TOU rates), and Ireland (all five suppliers must offer dynamic tariffs from June 2026). Markets like South Africa and Spain are better served by backup and solar self-consumption than by arbitrage.

Do I need solar for peak shaving?

No. Arbitrage works with grid charging alone — the battery fills from cheap off-peak grid power and discharges at peak. Solar adds free recharge and lifts self-consumption, but it is not required. See our no-solar backup + arbitrage calculator for the pure-grid case.

What equipment do I need for battery arbitrage?

A battery with an energy management system or hybrid inverter that supports AC grid charging in TOU mode, plus a TOU or dynamic tariff and a smart meter. Verify the inverter spec line 'AC grid charging (TOU mode)' before quoting arbitrage — some models self-consume solar but cannot actually charge from the grid on a schedule, which collapses the savings.
